What conformal RUL intervals deliver: an 80-cell audit of coverage, per-engine failure, and a conditional epistemic scale

Abstract

Remaining-useful-life (RUL) intervals are used to schedule maintenance, yet they are usually evaluated by marginal coverage on a single training run. We audit a frozen conformalized quantile regression pipeline on C-MAPSS at the resolution at which it is used: four subsets x five folds x four seeds = 80 cells, with an engine-disjoint calibration split. CQR lowers the Winkler score against a constant-width conformal baseline in 75/80 cells and 20/20 folds (mean -10.22%) at matched marginal coverage (-0.42 pp). The same cells contain engines whose intervals cover 12-49% of their windows, and 18/20 folds contain an engine below 0.80; the failure is repeatable across seeds (ICC 0.780). We then test whether a per-sample epistemic scale (MC-Dropout) repairs this. Against a same-centre constant-width control, the scale improves 19/20 cells on FD002 but only 1/20 on FD003; at the fold level, 9/10 multi-operating-condition folds improve versus 2/10 single-condition folds. A pre-registered calibration-side diagnostic fails its support criteria (-0.039 [-0.431, 0.317]), because with six calibration engines its own bootstrap standard deviation is 0.41-0.49. For practice, the results argue for reporting per-engine coverage and replicating across seeds before an interval is used in a maintenance decision.

1. Introduction

Remaining-useful-life (RUL) prognostics is one of the places where a prediction interval matters more than a point estimate: maintenance decisions are made against a bound, not against a mean. Conformal prediction has therefore become a natural tool for RUL, because it turns any point or quantile predictor into an interval with a distribution-free finite-sample guarantee (Lei et al., 2018; Vovk et al., 2005). Conformalized quantile regression (CQR) (Romano et al., 2019) is the variant most often used, since it lets the interval width follow the local difficulty of the prediction instead of being constant.

The guarantee, however, is marginal: it holds on average over calibration and test points. It says nothing about a single engine, and it says nothing about whether the interval is efficient. Both gaps matter in prognostics, where the unit of a maintenance decision is one engine. Recent work has begun to map these gaps — conditional coverage is impossible to guarantee distribution-free (Barber et al., 2021), group-conditional guarantees require group structure and enough per-group samples (Gibbs et al., 2025), calibration-set-conditional coverage is itself a random quantity (Bian & Barber, 2023), and label-free auditing of coverage under shift has a detectability boundary (Moussa, 2026). Yet the studies we reviewed for this paper typically report marginal coverage from a single training run.

This paper asks three questions about a frozen, published-style CQR pipeline for C-MAPSS RUL:

  • Q1. Is the reported efficiency gain — lower interval score than a constant-width conformal baseline — stable across training seeds and folds, and is it obtained at matched coverage?
  • Q2. When marginal coverage is close to nominal, what does the per-engine coverage distribution look like, and is the failure repeatable or is it training noise?
  • Q3. If we replace the learned half-width with a per-sample epistemic-uncertainty scale (MC-Dropout), when does it help, and can a calibration-side diagnostic tell us in advance?

The answers are organised as four contributions.

C1 — A controlled, multi-seed comparison. We evaluate 4 subsets × 5 folds × 4 seeds = 80 cells against a constant-width conformal baseline trained identically, at the nominal level. CQR lowers the Winkler score in 75/80 cells and in 20/20 folds (mean −10.22%), while realised coverage differs by −0.42 pp on average. We report the per-cell coverage spread (up to 8.84 pp) rather than hiding it.

C2 — The unit-level view. Per-engine coverage is heavy-tailed: the worst engine reaches 0.1217 (FD004) across the 80 cells, and 18/20 folds contain an engine below 0.80. Treating seeds as replicates, the intraclass correlation of per-engine coverage is 0.780, so the failure is a repeatable property of the engine under this partition, not sampling noise.

C3 — A controlled test of an epistemic scale. Comparing MC-Dropout against a constant-width interval built on the same MC mean isolates the scale from the centre shift. The effect is strongly heterogeneous: 19/20 cells improve on FD002 (mean −7.09%) while 1/20 improve on FD003 (mean +28.06%); at the fold level, 9/10 multi-condition folds improve versus 2/10 single-condition folds (Fisher exact p = 0.0055, descriptive).

C4 — A pre-registered diagnostic that fails, and why. We registered the natural hypothesis that the scale is useful exactly when it ranks calibration engines by difficulty. The hypothesis does not reach its pre-registered support criteria: the rank correlation between the diagnostic and the outcome is −0.039 [−0.431, +0.317]. The reason is measurable — with six calibration engines the diagnostic’s own bootstrap standard deviation is 0.41–0.49, so its 95% interval spans most of its range.

The common thread is a measurement point rather than a methodological one: marginal coverage and a single training seed are weak summaries of what a conformal RUL interval delivers. We do not propose a new interval method; we quantify what the current one gives, at the resolution at which it is used.

2. Related work

2.1 Conditional validity and its limits

Split conformal prediction guarantees marginal coverage under exchangeability (Lei et al., 2018; Vovk et al., 2005). Exact distribution-free conditional coverage is impossible without further assumptions (Barber et al., 2021), and coverage guarantees are conditional on the calibration set, hence random (Bian & Barber, 2023). Practical relaxations impose structure: group-conditional guarantees over a user-specified collection of subgroups (Gibbs et al., 2025), or weighting by an estimated density ratio under covariate shift (Tibshirani et al., 2019). Our work does not add to this theory; it measures how far a standard pipeline is from the conditional ideal, and it keeps the counting object explicit — calibration scores per cell, not calibration engines.

2.2 Conformal RUL

CQR (Romano et al., 2019) is the base of most recent conformal RUL work. CRULP (Piao et al., 2025) integrates a deep RUL predictor with an uncertainty-aware CQR variant built on epistemic uncertainty, using ensemble CQR and cross-conformal prediction. Robinson (Robinson, 2026) applies gradient-boosted point predictors with CQR to all four C-MAPSS subsets and adds a risk-aware decision layer; it reports empirical coverage of 88–92% at a 90% nominal level and per-engine degradation curves, but no per-engine coverage distribution. We use the same family of predictors and intervals, and we report the per-engine distribution.

2.3 Audits, conditional coverage diagnostics, and boundaries

Several 2026 works are close in spirit. Yan (Yan, 2026) audits conformal RUL intervals on C-MAPSS and N-CMAPSS along operating regimes and predicted-RUL bands, with ACI and Mondrian variants, and explains mid-band failures by band misassignment. Domingues and Ather (Domingues & Ather, 2026) document conditional collapse near end of life (61–90 cycles remaining; 37–50% near failure) on C-MAPSS. Zhou et al. (Zhou et al., 2026) reframe conditional-coverage assessment as a supervised learning problem: a reliability estimator predicts instance-level coverage, with convergence rates, but it requires a labeled evaluation set. Moussa (Moussa, 2026) establishes detectability bounds and a sharp identified set for marginal target coverage under shift, and states explicitly that the approach repairs marginal rather than conditional coverage. For sparse cells, HACC (Zou et al., 2026) gives an arithmetic threshold below which the empirical quantile does not exist, and repairs it by shrinkage.

2.4 What this paper adds

The difference is a controlled comparison, not a new estimator:

  1. Same-centre control. MC-Dropout changes the centre as well as the scale. Holding the centre fixed at the MC mean isolates the scale, which is what the question “is an epistemic scale useful?” actually asks.
  2. Multi-seed replication. Four seeds on a shared partition let us separate engine-level failure from training noise (ICC 0.780) and attach a sampling-noise floor (median 0.63 pp, p90 3.20 pp, 0/80 direction flips) to every subset-level statement.
  3. A pre-registered diagnostic test. The calibration-side diagnostic is specified, thresholded, and registered before it is evaluated, and its estimation variance is measured rather than assumed.
  4. Explicit counting objects. Calibration engines (6/16/6/15), calibration scores per cell, and per-engine windows are kept distinct throughout; conflating these three may make claims about “unit-level validity” harder to compare across papers.

We make no claim of theoretical novelty, and we do not claim to be the first to observe conditional coverage failure in RUL; that observation is due to (Domingues & Ather, 2026) and (Yan, 2026).

3. Experimental protocol

3.1 Data

We use the four C-MAPSS turbofan subsets FD001–FD004 (Saxena et al., 2008). They differ in the number of operating conditions (one for FD001/FD003, six for FD002/FD004) and in the number of fault modes (one for FD001/FD002, two for FD003/FD004). Training targets are the piecewise-linear remaining useful life clipped at 125 cycles. Windows of 50 cycles are built per engine; FD002/FD004 are condition-normalised with six operating-condition clusters fitted on training engines only.

The official C-MAPSS test files are not used anywhere in this paper. All results come from held-out engines inside the training data. This is a deliberate protocol choice: the question is how a conformal interval behaves on unseen engines drawn from the same population, not how it scores on a fixed test file.

3.2 Splits and seeds

Engines are partitioned into five folds by KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=20261104). Within each fold, 30% of the validation engines are reserved for conformal calibration and the rest form the test set; the split is engine-disjoint and fixed at seed 42 for every cell. The number of calibration engines is therefore 6 (FD001, FD003) and 16 / 15 (FD002, FD004); the corresponding test sets contain 14 / 36 / 14 / 35 engines.

Each fold is trained with four seeds (42, 7, 2024, 13), giving 4 subsets × 5 folds × 4 seeds = 80 cells. Seeds change only the initialisation and training trajectory; the engine partition is shared by all four seeds.

3.3 Frozen predictor and interval

The predictor is an IDA-PE backbone with a zero-initialised projection of two prior features and two output heads producing a centre cic_i and a non-negative half-width si=softplus(gϕ(zi))s_i=\mathrm{softplus}(g_\phi(z_i)):

qilo=ci−si,qihi=ci+si.q^{lo}_i = c_i - s_i, \qquad q^{hi}_i = c_i + s_i .

It is trained end-to-end with the pinball loss at 0.05/0.95 plus 0.1(ci−yi)20.1\,(c_i-y_i)^2. Calibration is split conformal on the CQR score Ei=max⁡(qilo−yi,yi−qihi)E_i=\max(q^{lo}_i-y_i,\ y_i-q^{hi}_i):

q̂=Quantile(1−δ)(1+1/n)({Ei}),C(x)=[qlo−q̂,qhi+q̂],\hat q = \mathrm{Quantile}_{(1-\delta)(1+1/n)}\big(\{E_i\}\big), \qquad C(x) = \big[q^{lo}-\hat q,\ q^{hi}+\hat q\big],

with δ=0.1\delta=0.1 and nn the number of calibration windows. The method is frozen before any of the analyses below; it is treated as a baseline, not as a contribution.

3.4 Four arms

Arm Centre Half-width Purpose
Constant width deterministic cic_i q̂\hat q (absolute-residual conformal) the frozen baseline the literature compares against
CQR cic_i si+q̂s_i+\hat q the method under audit
MC-Dropout scale mean of 30 stochastic forward passes q̂⋅sdi\hat q\cdot \mathrm{sd}_i a per-sample epistemic-uncertainty scale
Same-centre constant same MC mean q̂\hat q control: isolates the scale from the centre shift

The fourth arm is the key design element. MC-Dropout changes both the centre (to the MC mean) and the scale; comparing it with the deterministic-centre constant width confounds the two. Comparing it with the same-centre constant width attributes the difference to the scale alone.

3.5 Metrics

The primary metric is the Winkler interval score at a fixed α=0.1\alpha=0.1, mean[(u−l)+2α((l−y)++(y−u)+)]\mathrm{mean}\big[(u-l)+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}((l-y)_+ + (y-u)_+)\big], which penalises width and miscoverage jointly. We also report PICP (marginal coverage), PINAW, and the per-engine coverage distribution (minimum, 5th percentile, median). The engine, not the window, is the unit for all coverage statements: thousands of windows from a few engines are not independent samples.

3.6 Statistical discipline

The reporting unit is the fold (4 subsets × 5 folds = 20), with seeds averaged inside a fold. Three caveats are stated rather than corrected away:

  1. The 80 cells share one five-fold partition, so they are not 80 independent observations.
  2. The 20 folds share training data through cross-validation overlap, so we report descriptive statistics and bootstrap intervals, not independent-sample significance tests.
  3. Coverage-aligned comparisons interpolate each method’s frontier using test labels and therefore cannot represent a deployable selection rule; they appear in Appendix A as a post-hoc diagnostic only, clearly separated from the nominal-level results.

3.7 Reproducibility and integrity gates

Three executable gates precede every number in this paper:

  • Frozen baseline (scripts/freeze_audit.py, 50 assertions): re-derives the 80-cell table, the fold-level summaries, the seed-variance decomposition, the per-engine coverage tails, the permutation ablation and the cross-domain results from the stored predictions.
  • MC-Dropout integrity (scripts/verify_mcd80.py): the 40 cells that required retraining were re-run from scratch, and their point predictions reproduce the frozen ones bit-for-bit (max |Δylo|=0.0|\Delta y_{lo}| = 0.0).
  • Sampling noise floor (scripts/compare_mcd_draws.py): two independent MC-Dropout draws on the same checkpoints give a median absolute change of 0.63 pp, a 90th percentile of 3.20 pp, and a maximum of 14.70 pp on the primary metric, with 0/80 direction flips. Subset-level differences smaller than 3.20 pp are therefore reported as within noise.

The protocol is frozen before the analyses in Section 4; the pre-registered diagnostic test of Section 4.4 is registered in the project’s hypothesis log with its thresholds fixed in advance.

4. Results

4.1 The efficiency gain is reproducible, and it is not bought with coverage

At the nominal level (δ=0.1\delta=0.1), CQR lowers the Winkler score relative to the constant-width conformal baseline in 75 of 80 cells and in 20 of 20 folds; the cell-level mean change is −10.22% (median −11.30%).

The two arms do not trade coverage for sharpness. Averaged over the 80 cells, realised coverage is 0.8952 for CQR and 0.8994 for the constant-width arm, a difference of −0.42 pp; per subset the differences are −0.08, −0.43, −1.36 and +0.19 pp.  The match is a property of the subset means, not of every cell: the largest per-cell coverage difference is 8.84 pp. We state this explicitly because a reader who checks individual cells will find it.

We also verified that the comparison is not an artefact of a single training run: the four seeds share the engine partition, and 16 of 20 folds have all four seeds agreeing in sign, with a within-fold seed spread whose median is 6.13 pp and whose maximum is 36.63 pp (46.82% of the descriptive variance is within-fold).

4.2 Marginal coverage does not imply engine-level reliability

The per-engine coverage distribution is heavy-tailed downwards. Across the 80 cells, the lowest single-engine coverage is 0.4214 (FD001), 0.2375 (FD002), 0.4933 (FD003) and 0.1217 (FD004). 73 of the 80 cells contain at least one engine below 0.80, and in 18 of 20 folds the seed-averaged per-engine coverage falls below 0.80 for at least one engine.

The extremes are not isolated failures of one cell. Taking, for each cell, the minimum over its test engines and then the median of those minima across the 20 cells of a subset gives 0.6950 (FD001), 0.5438 (FD002), 0.7110 (FD003) and 0.5181 (FD004); the typical cell’s worst engine is itself well below nominal. All per-engine numbers in this section come from the 80-cell matrix and are recomputed under the same seed-42 calibration/test split for every cell, so they are directly comparable across seeds. The 20-fold count in the previous paragraph is the seed-averaged version of the same 80-cell computation, not a separate single-seed run.

This is not training noise. Treating the four seeds as replicates of the same engine under the same partition, the intraclass correlation of per-engine coverage is ICC(1,1) = 0.780, the mean pairwise Spearman correlation across seeds is +0.762 (99% of 120 seed pairs positive), the bottom-quartile engine sets overlap with a mean Jaccard of 0.599, and the within-engine standard deviation across seeds is 0.032 (population SD; the sample SD with 4 seeds is 0.0372). An engine that is under-covered by one training run tends to be under-covered by the others.

We stress what this does and does not show. It shows that, under this frozen partition and training configuration, the failure is a repeatable property of the engine. It does not show that the failure is intrinsic to the engine, that it transfers across partitions, or that it is identifiable at deployment time.

4.3 The epistemic scale helps or hurts depending on operating-condition structure

Replacing the learned half-width with a per-sample MC-Dropout scale changes the Winkler score by +9.49% on average over the 80 cells, with a median of −0.97%. The mean and the median point in opposite directions, so neither “better” nor “worse” is an accurate one-line summary. Two facts constrain the reading: the median is the same order as the sampling noise (median absolute change 0.63 pp), and the mean is dominated by a small number of catastrophic cells.

The split is by subset (same-centre control, so only the scale differs):

Subset Cells better Mean change Median change
FD001 (single OC) 8/20 +18.77% +10.65%
FD002 (multi OC) 19/20 −7.09% −7.29%
FD003 (single OC) 1/20 +28.06% +24.99%
FD004 (multi OC) 15/20 −1.78% −2.58%

FD002 and FD003 are both far outside the noise floor (p90 = 3.20 pp); FD004’s −1.78% is not, and we do not treat it as evidence of an improvement.

Aggregating to the fold level makes the structure sharper. Of the ten folds from multi-operating- condition subsets, 9 are improved (mean −4.44%); of the ten folds from single-condition subsets, 2 are improved (mean +23.41%); Fisher’s exact two-sided test on this 2×2 table gives p = 0.0055. The test is descriptive: the 20 folds share training data and are not independent draws, so the p-value quantifies the strength of the separation in this design, not a population-level effect.

4.4 A natural calibration-side diagnostic does not survive its own pre-registered test

Before looking at any of the above as a mechanism, we registered a hypothesis: that the scale is useful exactly when it ranks calibration engines by difficulty, measured by ρcal=Spearman{(s‾i,a‾i)}i∈calib\rho_{cal}=\mathrm{Spearman}\{(\bar s_i,\ \bar a_i)\}_{i\in\text{calib}}, the rank correlation between an engine’s mean dropout scale and its mean absolute centre error, computed on calibration engines only. Four gates were fixed in advance; three fail.

Gate Statistic Threshold Result
G-A Spearman(ρcal,rscale)\mathrm{Spearman}(\rho_{cal},\ r_{scale}) over 20 folds ≤ −0.35, CI upper < 0 −0.039 [−0.431, +0.317] — fail
G-B ρcal\rho_{cal} multi-OC minus single-OC ≥ 0.20, ≥4/5 folds +0.216, 4/5 — pass
G-C Spearman(scale shift,coverage gap)\mathrm{Spearman}(\text{scale shift},\ \text{coverage gap}) ≤ −0.30, CI upper < 0 −0.221 [−0.712, +0.300] — fail
G-D go/no-go rule at τ=0.5\tau=0.5 (fixed) mean ≤ −2%, FD003 ≤ +2% +2.79%, FD003 +13.77% — fail

The failure has a measurable cause. Bootstrapping engines within each cell (post-hoc; not part of the pre-registered gates), the sampling standard deviation of ρcal\rho_{cal} is 0.490 (FD001) and 0.414 (FD003), where only 6 engines are available for calibration, against 0.249 (FD002) and 0.171 (FD004) with 15–16. The corresponding per-cell 95% half-widths are 0.898, 0.479, 0.747 and 0.324. With six calibration engines the diagnostic’s own uncertainty spans most of its range, so a rule built on it cannot be reliably calibrated at this sample size — which is what G-A and G-D show.

We read this as an estimation result, not an impossibility result. It does not show that no calibration-side diagnostic can work; it shows that this one, at this calibration size, cannot. The distinction matters for practice: the usual way to justify a conditional scale is to check that it correlates with residuals on the calibration set, and that check is uninformative here.

4.5 The other candidate scales are not better

Two further comparisons place the dropout scale in context.

Against the learned conditional width. On the same 80 cells, the MC-Dropout arm is worse than CQR in 72 of 80 cells and in 20 of 20 folds, with a mean change of +21.42% and a median of +15.31%. On this comparison the learned half-width is better; we do not claim that it captures “more” of any particular quantity.

Against the prior-uncertainty scale. Permuting the prior standard deviation σT\sigma_T across windows — which destroys any association between the scale and the sample while keeping its marginal distribution — changes the Winkler score by +0.246% on average, with 11 of 20 folds becoming worse and FD004 improving by 3.02%. The permutation is within noise, so we do not treat σT\sigma_T as a useful conditional scale.

Cross-domain. On six run-to-full-failure FEMTO bearings (Nectoux et al., 2012), with the conformal quantile calibrated on 300 held-out windows per bearing and evaluated on 300 disjoint test windows, CQR improves on the constant-width baseline in 6 of 6 bearings with a mean change of −16.30%. For the record, an earlier internal evaluation of the same dumps calibrated and evaluated on the same windows and scored each frontier point at its own α\alpha; it gave 5/6 and −12.21%. It is noted only to explain the difference from earlier project records and is not used as evidence here. The split protocol above supports the efficiency result of Section 4.1 only; it is too small a sample to say anything about the scale or the per-engine findings.

5. Discussion

5.1 What the three results say together

Read separately, each result is unremarkable: conformal intervals can be sharper than constant-width ones; marginal coverage does not imply conditional coverage; an uncertainty scale can be unstable. Read together, they describe a specific gap between what is reported and what is used.

The efficiency result (Section 4.1) is robust — 20/20 folds, −10.22% — and it is obtained at matched coverage. That is a genuine and reproducible property of the pipeline. But the same 80 cells contain engines whose intervals cover 12–49% of their windows (Section 4.2). A practitioner reading only the marginal number would conclude that the interval is usable on a new engine; the per-engine distribution says that for some engines it is not, and that this is not a fluke of one training run.

The scale experiment (Section 4.3) then shows that the natural remedy — condition the width on an epistemic-uncertainty estimate — does not have a uniform effect. It is not that the scale is useless; on the multi-operating-condition subsets it improves 19/20 cells of FD002. It is that the sign of the effect depends on the subset, and the subset structure that separates the signs is operating-condition heterogeneity, not fault-mode count: FD002 and FD004 are multi-condition, FD001 and FD003 single-condition, and the fold-level split is 9/10 versus 2/10. We state this as an observed association; the experiment does not identify the mechanism, and we do not claim it does.

The pre-registered diagnostic (Section 4.4) closes the loop in a way that is useful precisely because it fails. The obvious way to justify a conditional scale is to check, on calibration engines, that it ranks them by difficulty. That check is not informative here: its correlation with the outcome is −0.039, and its own sampling standard deviation at six calibration engines is 0.41–0.49. The practical lesson is narrow: for this statistic (ρcal\rho_{cal}), at the calibration sizes of this five-fold C-MAPSS protocol (6–16 engines), the estimate is too noisy to support a go/no-go decision, even though the underlying idea is reasonable.

5.2 Implications for reporting practice

Three reporting choices would have changed the picture in this paper, and each is cheap:

  1. Report the per-engine coverage distribution, not only the mean. The marginal number is 0.895; the 1st percentile of per-engine coverage is 0.41–0.54 depending on subset, and the minimum is 0.12.
  2. Report at least two training seeds. With one seed, a 3.2 pp difference is inside the sampling noise of the scale experiment; with four seeds on a shared partition, the sign split is consistent across folds (9/10 versus 2/10), and two independent MC-Dropout draws change the sign in 0 of 80 cells, so the comparison itself is reproducible even though the seed spread is large.
  3. Use a same-centre control when the scale arm also changes the centre. Otherwise the comparison attributes a centre shift to the scale.

None of these requires new theory or additional data collection.

5.3 Relation to prior audits

Our per-engine finding is consistent with the conditional-collapse results of (Domingues & Ather, 2026) and the band-failure analysis of (Yan, 2026); we cite them as the source of the phenomenon and do not claim to have discovered it. What we add is the controlled scale comparison and the replication structure. Relative to (Zhou et al., 2026), the object differs: CPA estimates instance-level coverage from a labeled evaluation set, whereas we ask whether a calibration-side diagnostic is estimable at all before deployment. Relative to (Moussa, 2026), the claim is weaker by design: we offer no detectability bound, only a measured estimation variance for one named diagnostic under one frozen protocol.

6. Limitations

  • Dependence. The 80 cells share one five-fold partition, and the 20 folds share training data through cross-validation overlap. All intervals reported here are descriptive; no independent-sample significance test is claimed, including the Fisher exact test in Section 4.3, which quantifies the separation within this design only.
  • Single backbone, four subsets. The predictor is one IDA-PE configuration; the conclusions about the scale’s heterogeneity are established for this configuration and these four subsets, not for conformal RUL in general.
  • Cross-domain evidence is limited. The FEMTO comparison covers six run-to-full-failure bearings and supports only the efficiency claim, not the scale or unit-level findings.
  • Official test data are unused. This is a protocol choice: the question concerns unseen engines from the training population. It also means the numbers are not comparable to papers that report official-test scores.
  • The diagnostic’s variance is measured at three calibration sizes (6, 15, 16). We do not extrapolate a sample-size requirement from them.
  • No deployment claim. Nothing here shows that a failing engine can be identified before its labels arrive; Section 4.4 is evidence against one specific attempt, not a general impossibility result.

7. Conclusion

A frozen CQR pipeline for C-MAPSS RUL delivers a reproducible efficiency gain over a constant-width conformal baseline — 20/20 folds, −10.22% Winkler at matched marginal coverage — and at the same time leaves some engines covered as little as 12% of the time, a failure that is stable across training seeds. Replacing the learned half-width with an MC-Dropout scale does not fix this uniformly: its effect changes sign with operating-condition structure, and the natural calibration-side diagnostic for deciding when to use it does not survive a pre-registered test, because at six calibration engines the diagnostic is noisier than the signal it is meant to carry. The practical recommendation is measurement-oriented: report per-engine coverage, replicate across seeds, and control the centre when evaluating a scale.

Appendix A. Coverage-aligned comparison (post-hoc diagnostic)

This appendix is deliberately separated from the main results. The aligned comparison interpolates each method’s coverage–width frontier using test labels; it therefore cannot represent a deployable selection rule and is not used to support any claim in Sections 4–7.

For completeness we report the comparison at matched realised coverage (target 0.90, Winkler evaluated at a fixed α=0.1\alpha=0.1). CQR improves on the constant-width baseline in 75/80 cells and 19/20 folds, with a cell-level mean change of −9.09% and a fold-level median of −9.69%. The per-subset means are −7.57%, −14.07%, −6.49% and −8.22%.

The same diagnostic applied to the MC-Dropout arm gives 31/80 cells, +18.9% mean and +4.5% median — the opposite sign to the nominal-level result of Section 4.3. The reversal is expected: an arm that under-covers at the nominal level is widened to reach 0.90, and widening is penalised. We report it so that the two protocols are not confused, and we draw no conclusion from it.
