Abstract
Remaining-useful-life (RUL) intervals are used to sched-
ule maintenance, yet they are usually evaluated by marginal
coverage on a single training run. We audit a frozen con-
formalized quantile regression pipeline on C-MAPSS at
the resolution at which it is used: four subsets x five
folds x four seeds = 80 cells, with an engine-disjoint cal-
ibration split. CQR lowers the Winkler score against
a constant-width conformal baseline in 75/80 cells and
20/20 folds (mean -10.22%) at matched marginal cov-
erage (-0.42 pp). The same cells contain engines whose
intervals cover 12-49% of their windows, and 18/20 folds
contain an engine below 0.80; the failure is repeatable
across seeds (ICC 0.780). We then test whether a per-
sample epistemic scale (MC-Dropout) repairs this. Against
a same-centre constant-width control, the scale improves
19/20 cells on FD002 but only 1/20 on FD003; at the fold
level, 9/10 multi-operating-condition folds improve ver-
sus 2/10 single-condition folds. A pre-registered calibration-
side diagnostic fails its support criteria (-0.039 [-0.431,
0.317]), because with six calibration engines its own boot-
strap standard deviation is 0.41-0.49. For practice, the
results argue for reporting per-engine coverage and repli-
cating across seeds before an interval is used in a main-
tenance decision.
1. Introduction
Remaining-useful-life (RUL) prognostics is one of the
places where a prediction interval matters more than a
point estimate: maintenance decisions are made against
a bound, not against a mean. Conformal prediction
has therefore become a natural tool for RUL, because
it turns any point or quantile predictor into an interval
with a distribution-free finite-sample guarantee (Vovk,
Gammerman, et al., 2005; Lei, G’Sell, et al., 2018). Con-

formalized quantile regression (CQR) (Romano, Patter-
son, et al., 2019) is the variant most often used, since it
lets the interval width follow the local diﬀiculty of the
prediction instead of being constant.
The guarantee, however, is marginal: it holds on average
over calibration and test points. It says nothing about
a single engine, and it says nothing about whether the
interval is eﬀicient. Both gaps matter in prognostics,
where the unit of a maintenance decision is one engine.
Recent work has begun to map these gaps — condi-
tional coverage is impossible to guarantee distribution-
free (Barber, Candès, et al., 2021), group-conditional
guarantees require group structure and enough per-group
samples (Gibbs, Cherian, et al., 2025), calibration-set-
conditional coverage is itself a random quantity (Bian &
Barber, 2023), and label-free auditing of coverage under
shift has a detectability boundary (Moussa, 2026). Yet
the studies we reviewed for this paper typically report
marginal coverage from a single training run.
This paper asks three questions about a frozen, published-
style CQR pipeline for C-MAPSS RUL:
• Q1. Is the reported eﬀiciency gain — lower inter-
val score than a constant-width conformal baseline
— stable across training seeds and folds, and is it
obtained at matched coverage?
• Q2. When marginal coverage is close to nominal,
what does the per-engine coverage distribution look
like, and is the failure repeatable or is it training
noise?
• Q3. If we replace the learned half-width with a per-
sample epistemic-uncertainty scale (MC-Dropout),
when does it help, and can a calibration-side diag-
nostic tell us in advance?
The answers are organised as four contributions.
C1 — A controlled, multi-seed comparison. We eval-
uate 4 subsets × 5 folds × 4 seeds = 80 cells against
a constant-width conformal baseline trained identically,

We registered the natural hypothesis that the scale is
useful exactly when it ranks calibration engines by dif-
ficulty. The hypothesis does not reach its pre-registered
support criteria: the rank correlation between the diag-
nostic and the outcome is −0.039 [−0.431, +0.317]. The
reason is measurable — with six calibration engines the
diagnostic’s own bootstrap standard deviation is 0.41–
0.49, so its 95% interval spans most of its range.
The common thread is a measurement point rather than
a methodological one: marginal coverage and a single
training seed are weak summaries of what a conformal
RUL interval delivers. We do not propose a new interval
method; we quantify what the current one gives, at the
resolution at which it is used.
2. Related work
2.1. Conditional validity and its limits
Split conformal prediction guarantees marginal cover-
age under exchangeability (Vovk, Gammerman, et al.,
2005; Lei, G’Sell, et al., 2018). Exact distribution-free
conditional coverage is impossible without further as-
sumptions (Barber, Candès, et al., 2021), and coverage
guarantees are conditional on the calibration set, hence
random (Bian & Barber, 2023). Practical relaxations
impose structure: group-conditional guarantees over a
user-specified collection of subgroups (Gibbs, Cherian,
et al., 2025), or weighting by an estimated density ratio
under covariate shift (Tibshirani, Barber, et al., 2019).
Our work does not add to this theory; it measures how
far a standard pipeline is from the conditional ideal, and
it keeps the counting object explicit — calibration scores
per cell, not calibration engines.
2.2. Conformal RUL
CQR (Romano, Patterson, et al., 2019) is the base of
most recent conformal RUL work. CRULP (Piao, Huang,
et al., 2025) integrates a deep RUL predictor with an
uncertainty-aware CQR variant built on epistemic un-
certainty, using ensemble CQR and cross-conformal pre-
diction. Robinson (Robinson, 2026) applies gradient-
boosted point predictors with CQR to all four C-MAPSS
subsets and adds a risk-aware decision layer; it reports
empirical coverage of 88–92% at a 90% nominal level and
per-engine degradation curves, but no per-engine cover-
age distribution. We use the same family of predictors
and intervals, and we report the per-engine distribution.
2.3. Audits, conditional coverage diagnostics, and bound-
aries
Several 2026 works are close in spirit. Yan (Yan, 2026)
audits conformal RUL intervals on C-MAPSS and N-
CMAPSS along operating regimes and predicted-RUL
bands, with ACI and Mondrian variants, and explains
mid-band failures by band misassignment. Domingues
and Ather (Domingues & Ather, 2026) document con-
ditional collapse near end of life (61–90 cycles remain-
ing; 37–50% near failure) on C-MAPSS. Zhou et al.
(Zhou, Zhang, et al., 2026) reframe conditional-coverage
assessment as a supervised learning problem: a reliabil-
ity estimator predicts instance-level coverage, with con-
vergence rates, but it requires a labeled evaluation set.
Moussa (Moussa, 2026) establishes detectability bounds
and a sharp identified set for marginal target coverage
under shift, and states explicitly that the approach re-
pairs marginal rather than conditional coverage. For
sparse cells, HACC (Zou, Feng, et al., 2026) gives an
arithmetic threshold below which the empirical quantile
does not exist, and repairs it by shrinkage.
2.4. What this paper adds
The difference is a controlled comparison, not a new es-
timator:
1. Same-centre control. MC-Dropout changes the cen-
tre as well as the scale. Holding the centre fixed
at the MC mean isolates the scale, which is what
the question “is an epistemic scale useful?” actually
asks.
2. Multi-seed replication. Four seeds on a shared parti-
tion let us separate engine-level failure from training
noise (ICC 0.780) and attach a sampling-noise floor
(median 0.63 pp, p90 3.20 pp, 0/80 direction flips)
to every subset-level statement.

3. Experimental protocol
3.1. Data
We use the four C-MAPSS turbofan subsets FD001–
FD004 (Saxena, Goebel, et al., 2008). They differ in the
number of operating conditions (one for FD001/FD003,
six for FD002/FD004) and in the number of fault modes
(one for FD001/FD002, two for FD003/FD004). Train-
ing targets are the piecewise-linear remaining useful life
clipped at 125 cycles. Windows of 50 cycles are built
per engine; FD002/FD004 are condition-normalised with
six operating-condition clusters fitted on training engines
only.
The oﬀicial C-MAPSS test files are not used anywhere
in this paper. All results come from held-out engines
inside the training data. This is a deliberate protocol
choice: the question is how a conformal interval behaves
on unseen engines drawn from the same population, not
how it scores on a fixed test file.
3.2. Splits and seeds
Engines are partitioned into five folds by KFold(n_splits=5,
shuffle=True, random_state=20261104). Within each
fold, 30% of the validation engines are reserved for con-
formal calibration and the rest form the test set; the
split is engine-disjoint and fixed at seed 42 for every cell.
The number of calibration engines is therefore 6 (FD001,
FD003) and 16 / 15 (FD002, FD004); the corresponding
test sets contain 14 / 36 / 14 / 35 engines.
Each fold is trained with four seeds (42, 7, 2024, 13),
giving 4 subsets × 5 folds × 4 seeds = 80 cells. Seeds
change only the initialisation and training trajectory; the
engine partition is shared by all four seeds.

3.4. Four arms
The fourth arm is the key design element. MC-Dropout
changes both the centre (to the MC mean) and the scale;
comparing it with the deterministic-centre constant width
confounds the two. Comparing it with the same-centre
constant width attributes the difference to the scale alone.
3.5. Metrics
The primary metric is the Winkler interval score at a
fixed α = 0 .1, mean

which penalises width and miscoverage jointly. We also
report PICP (marginal coverage), PINA W, and the per-
engine coverage distribution (minimum, 5th percentile,
median). The engine, not the window, is the unit for all
coverage statements: thousands of windows from a few
engines are not independent samples.
3.6. Statistical discipline
The reporting unit is the fold (4 subsets × 5 folds =
20), with seeds averaged inside a fold. Three caveats are
stated rather than corrected away:
1. The 80 cells share one five-fold partition, so they are
not 80 independent observations.
2. The 20 folds share training data through cross-validation
overlap, so we report descriptive statistics and boot-
strap intervals, not independent-sample significance
tests.
3. Coverage-aligned comparisons interpolate each method’s
frontier using test labels and therefore cannot rep-
resent a deployable selection rule; they appear in
Appendix A as a post-hoc diagnostic only, clearly
separated from the nominal-level results.

4. Results
4.1. The eﬀiciency gain is reproducible, and it is not
bought with coverage
At the nominal level ( δ = 0.1), CQR lowers the Winkler
score relative to the constant-width conformal baseline
in 75 of 80 cells and in 20 of 20 folds; the cell-level mean
change is −10.22% (median −11.30%).
The two arms do not trade coverage for sharpness. A v-
eraged over the 80 cells, realised coverage is 0.8952 for
CQR and 0.8994 for the constant-width arm, a differ-
ence of −0.42 pp; per subset the differences are −0.08,
−0.43, −1.36 and +0.19 pp. The match is a property of
the subset means, not of every cell: the largest per-cell
coverage difference is 8.84 pp. We state this explicitly
because a reader who checks individual cells will find it.
We also verified that the comparison is not an artefact
of a single training run: the four seeds share the engine
partition, and 16 of 20 folds have all four seeds agreeing
in sign, with a within-fold seed spread whose median is
6.13 pp and whose maximum is 36.63 pp (46.82% of the
descriptive variance is within-fold).
4.2. Marginal coverage does not imply engine-level reli-
ability
The per-engine coverage distribution is heavy-tailed down-
wards. Across the 80 cells, the lowest single-engine cov-
erage is 0.4214 (FD001), 0.2375 (FD002), 0.4933 (FD003)
and 0.1217 (FD004). 73 of the 80 cells contain at least
one engine below 0.80, and in 18 of 20 folds the seed-
averaged per-engine coverage falls below 0.80 for at least
one engine.
The extremes are not isolated failures of one cell. Tak-
ing, for each cell, the minimum over its test engines and
then the median of those minima across the 20 cells of
a subset gives 0.6950 (FD001), 0.5438 (FD002), 0.7110
(FD003) and 0.5181 (FD004); the typical cell’s worst
engine is itself well below nominal. All per-engine num-
bers in this section come from the 80-cell matrix and
are recomputed under the same seed-42 calibration/test
split for every cell, so they are directly comparable across
seeds. The 20-fold count in the previous paragraph is the
seed-averaged version of the same 80-cell computation,
not a separate single-seed run.
This is not training noise. Treating the four seeds as
replicates of the same engine under the same partition,
the intraclass correlation of per-engine coverage is ICC(1,1)
= 0.780, the mean pairwise Spearman correlation across
seeds is +0.762 (99% of 120 seed pairs positive), the
bottom-quartile engine sets overlap with a mean Jac-
card of 0.599, and the within-engine standard deviation
across seeds is 0.032 (population SD; the sample SD with
4 seeds is 0.0372). An engine that is under-covered by
one training run tends to be under-covered by the others.
We stress what this does and does not show. It shows
that, under this frozen partition and training configura-
tion, the failure is a repeatable property of the engine. It
does not show that the failure is intrinsic to the engine,
that it transfers across partitions, or that it is identifi-
able at deployment time.

4.3. The epistemic scale helps or hurts depending on
operating-condition structure
Replacing the learned half-width with a per-sample MC-
Dropout scale changes the Winkler score by +9.49% on
average over the 80 cells, with a median of −0.97%. The
mean and the median point in opposite directions, so nei-
ther “better” nor “worse” is an accurate one-line sum-
mary. Two facts constrain the reading: the median is
the same order as the sampling noise (median absolute
change 0.63 pp), and the mean is dominated by a small
number of catastrophic cells.
The split is by subset (same-centre control, so only the
scale differs):
FD002 and FD003 are both far outside the noise floor
(p90 = 3.20 pp); FD004’s −1.78% is not, and we do not
treat it as evidence of an improvement.
Aggregating to the fold level makes the structure sharper.
Of the ten folds from multi-operating- condition subsets,
9 are improved (mean −4.44%); of the ten folds from
single-condition subsets, 2 are improved (mean +23.41%);
Fisher’s exact two-sided test on this 2×2 table gives p =
0.0055. The test is descriptive: the 20 folds share train-
ing data and are not independent draws, so the p-value
quantifies the strength of the separation in this design,
not a population-level effect.
4.4. A natural calibration-side diagnostic does not sur-
vive its own pre-registered test
Before looking at any of the above as a mechanism, we
registered a hypothesis: that the scale is useful exactly
when it ranks calibration engines by diﬀiculty, measured
by ρcal = Spearman {(¯si, ¯ai)}i∈calib, the rank correlation
between an engine’s mean dropout scale and its mean
absolute centre error, computed on calibration engines
only. Four gates were fixed in advance; three fail.
The failure has a measurable cause. Bootstrapping en-
gines within each cell (post-hoc; not part of the pre-
registered gates), the sampling standard deviation of ρcal
is 0.490 (FD001) and 0.414 (FD003), where only 6 en-
gines are available for calibration, against 0.249 (FD002)
and 0.171 (FD004) with 15–16. The corresponding per-
cell 95% half-widths are 0.898, 0.479, 0.747 and 0.324.
With six calibration engines the diagnostic’s own uncer-
tainty spans most of its range, so a rule built on it cannot
be reliably calibrated at this sample size — which is what
G-A and G-D show.
We read this as an estimation result, not an impossibility
result. It does not show that no calibration-side diagnos-
tic can work; it shows that this one, at this calibration
size, cannot. The distinction matters for practice: the
usual way to justify a conditional scale is to check that it
correlates with residuals on the calibration set, and that
check is uninformative here.
4.5. The other candidate scales are not better
Two further comparisons place the dropout scale in con-
text.
Against the learned conditional width. On the same 80
cells, the MC-Dropout arm is worse than CQR in 72
of 80 cells and in 20 of 20 folds, with a mean change of
+21.42% and a median of +15.31%. On this comparison
the learned half-width is better; we do not claim that it
captures “more” of any particular quantity.
Against the prior-uncertainty scale. Permuting the prior
standard deviation σT across windows — which destroys
any association between the scale and the sample while
keeping its marginal distribution — changes the Win-
kler score by +0.246% on average, with 11 of 20 folds
becoming worse and FD004 improving by 3.02%. The
permutation is within noise, so we do not treat σT as a
useful conditional scale.
Cross-domain. On six run-to-full-failure FEMTO bear-
ings (Nectoux, Gouriveau, et al., 2012), with the con-
formal quantile calibrated on 300 held-out windows per
bearing and evaluated on 300 disjoint test windows, CQR
improves on the constant-width baseline in 6 of 6 bear-
ings with a mean change of −16.30%. For the record, an
earlier internal evaluation of the same dumps calibrated
and evaluated on the same windows and scored each
frontier point at its own α; it gave 5/6 and −12.21%.
It is noted only to explain the difference from earlier
project records and is not used as evidence here. The
split protocol above supports the eﬀiciency result of Sec-
tion 4.1 only; it is too small a sample to say anything
about the scale or the per-engine findings.
5. Discussion
5.1. What the three results say together
Read separately, each result is unremarkable: confor-
mal intervals can be sharper than constant-width ones;
marginal coverage does not imply conditional coverage;
an uncertainty scale can be unstable. Read together,
they describe a specific gap between what is reported
and what is used.
The eﬀiciency result (Section 4.1) is robust — 20/20
folds, −10.22% — and it is obtained at matched cover-
age. That is a genuine and reproducible property of the
pipeline. But the same 80 cells contain engines whose
intervals cover 12–49% of their windows (Section 4.2).

The scale experiment (Section 4.3) then shows that the

It is not that the scale is useless; on the multi-operating-

that the sign of the effect depends on the subset, and the
subset structure that separates the signs is operating-
condition heterogeneity, not fault-mode count: FD002
and FD004 are multi-condition, FD001 and FD003 single-
condition, and the fold-level split is 9/10 versus 2/10.
We state this as an observed association; the experiment
does not identify the mechanism, and we do not claim it
does.
The pre-registered diagnostic (Section 4.4) closes the
loop in a way that is useful precisely because it fails.
The obvious way to justify a conditional scale is to check,
on calibration engines, that it ranks them by diﬀiculty.
That check is not informative here: its correlation with
the outcome is −0.039, and its own sampling standard
deviation at six calibration engines is 0.41–0.49. The
practical lesson is narrow: for this statistic ( ρcal), at
the calibration sizes of this five-fold C-MAPSS protocol
(6–16 engines), the estimate is too noisy to support a
go/no-go decision, even though the underlying idea is
reasonable.
5.2. Implications for reporting practice
Three reporting choices would have changed the picture
in this paper, and each is cheap:
1. Report the per-engine coverage distribution, not only
the mean. The marginal number is 0.895; the 1st
percentile of per-engine coverage is 0.41–0.54 de-
pending on subset, and the minimum is 0.12.
2. Report at least two training seeds. With one seed, a
3.2 pp difference is inside the sampling noise of the
scale experiment; with four seeds on a shared par-
tition, the sign split is consistent across folds (9/10
versus 2/10), and two independent MC-Dropout draws
change the sign in 0 of 80 cells, so the comparison
itself is reproducible even though the seed spread is
large.
3. Use a same-centre control when the scale arm also
changes the centre. Otherwise the comparison at-
tributes a centre shift to the scale.
None of these requires new theory or additional data
collection.
5.3. Relation to prior audits
Our per-engine finding is consistent with the conditional-
collapse results of (Domingues & Ather, 2026) and the
band-failure analysis of (Yan, 2026); we cite them as
the source of the phenomenon and do not claim to have
discovered it. What we add is the controlled scale com-
parison and the replication structure. Relative to (Zhou,
Zhang, et al., 2026), the object differs: CPA estimates
instance-level coverage from a labeled evaluation set,
whereas we ask whether a calibration-side diagnostic is
estimable at all before deployment. Relative to (Moussa,
2026), the claim is weaker by design: we offer no de-
tectability bound, only a measured estimation variance
for one named diagnostic under one frozen protocol.
6. Limitations
• Dependence. The 80 cells share one five-fold parti-
tion, and the 20 folds share training data through
cross-validation overlap. All intervals reported here
are descriptive; no independent-sample significance
test is claimed, including the Fisher exact test in
Section 4.3, which quantifies the separation within
this design only.
• Single backbone, four subsets. The predictor is one

IDA-PE configuration; the conclusions about the scale’s
heterogeneity are established for this configuration
and these four subsets, not for conformal RUL in
general.
• Cross-domain evidence is limited. The FEMTO com-
parison covers six run-to-full-failure bearings and
supports only the eﬀiciency claim, not the scale or
unit-level findings.
• Oﬀicial test data are unused. This is a protocol
choice: the question concerns unseen engines from
the training population. It also means the numbers
are not comparable to papers that report oﬀicial-test
scores.
• The diagnostic’s variance is measured at three cal-
ibration sizes (6, 15, 16). We do not extrapolate a
sample-size requirement from them.
• No deployment claim. Nothing here shows that a
failing engine can be identified before its labels ar-
rive; Section 4.4 is evidence against one specific at-
tempt, not a general impossibility result.
7. Conclusion
A frozen CQR pipeline for C-MAPSS RUL delivers a re-
producible eﬀiciency gain over a constant-width confor-
mal baseline — 20/20 folds, −10.22% Winkler at matched
marginal coverage — and at the same time leaves some
engines covered as little as 12% of the time, a failure that
is stable across training seeds. Replacing the learned
half-width with an MC-Dropout scale does not fix this
uniformly: its effect changes sign with operating-condition
structure, and the natural calibration-side diagnostic for
deciding when to use it does not survive a pre-registered
test, because at six calibration engines the diagnostic is
noisier than the signal it is meant to carry. The prac-
tical recommendation is measurement-oriented: report
per-engine coverage, replicate across seeds, and control
the centre when evaluating a scale.
8. Appendix A. Coverage-aligned comparison (post-hoc
diagnostic)
This appendix is deliberately separated from the
main results. The aligned comparison interpo-
lates each method’s coverage–width frontier us-
ing test labels; it therefore cannot represent a
deployable selection rule and is not used to sup-
port any claim in Sections 4–7.
For completeness we report the comparison at matched
realised coverage (target 0.90, Winkler evaluated at a
fixed α = 0 .1). CQR improves on the constant-width
baseline in 75/80 cells and 19/20 folds, with a cell-level
mean change of −9.09% and a fold-level median of −9.69%.
The per-subset means are −7.57%, −14.07%, −6.49%
and −8.22%.
The same diagnostic applied to the MC-Dropout arm
gives 31/80 cells, +18.9% mean and +4.5% median —
the opposite sign to the nominal-level result of Section
4.3. The reversal is expected: an arm that under-covers
at the nominal level is widened to reach 0.90, and widen-
ing is penalised. We report it so that the two protocols
are not confused, and we draw no conclusion from it.
